Getting a hospital bill can feel like getting a number you have no choice but to accept.

You don't.

Do not assume the first number you see is the final number.

A hospital bill reflects a billing process that can involve the hospital, your insurer, negotiated rates, financial-assistance policies, and federal or state protections. There are several things you can check before deciding how to handle the balance.

If you're trying to figure out how to negotiate a hospital bill, this is the order we'd work through.

> **Important:** This article provides general consumer and price information, not medical, legal, tax, or individualized financial advice. Never delay medically necessary care because of a billing dispute. Hospital policies, insurance coverage, deadlines, collection practices, and state protections vary.

## Step 0: Don't rush to pay it—and don't ignore it

Two common mistakes are paying a bill before reviewing it and ignoring it until the account becomes harder to resolve in collections.

Instead, contact the hospital and explain that you're reviewing the bill and want to understand your options.

You can ask what deadlines apply and whether the account can be placed on hold while you review the charges. Don't assume that a phone call automatically stops collection activity; ask the hospital what its policy is and keep a record of what you're told.

The goal is simple: give yourself time to understand what you're being asked to pay without pretending the bill doesn't exist.

## Step 1: Get the itemized hospital bill

If the statement you received only shows a few totals or broad descriptions, ask for a fully itemized statement.

Try:

> "Please send me a fully itemized bill showing the individual charges and billing codes."

The itemized version gives you something concrete to review.

Look for things such as:

- Duplicate charges
- Incorrect quantities
- Services you don't recognize
- Tests or procedures that were cancelled
- Room charges that don't match your dates of care
- Charges dated after discharge
- Incorrect insurance or patient information

You don't need medical-billing expertise to question something that doesn't look right.

Try:

> "Can you explain this charge? I don't believe I received this service."

You're asking for an explanation rather than making an accusation.

We are deliberately not using a broad statistic about the percentage of medical bills that contain errors. Those figures are often repeated without a clear, current source. The useful takeaway is simpler: reviewing an itemized bill gives you a better basis for identifying and questioning individual charges.

## Step 2: Check whether a surprise-billing protection applies

Before treating the balance as a normal bill to negotiate, check whether a federal or state billing protection applies to your situation.

The federal [**No Surprises Act**](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/no-surprises-act) provides protections for many people with employer or individual health insurance against certain unexpected out-of-network bills, including many emergency services and certain out-of-network services at in-network facilities.

If you are uninsured or are not using insurance, there are also federal requirements around [Good Faith Estimates](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights/help/guides/good-faith-estimate) for scheduled services.

For example, [CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights/know-your-rights/no-insurance) says that if you are uninsured or choose not to use insurance and the final bill from a provider is at least **$400 more than the provider's Good Faith Estimate**, you may be able to use the federal patient-provider dispute process.

That is different from negotiating. If a formal billing protection applies, you may have a dispute process available rather than simply asking the hospital for a lower price.

## Step 3: Apply for financial assistance

This is one of the most important things to check, particularly if you're dealing with a tax-exempt hospital.

Under [Section 501(r) of the Internal Revenue Code](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/general-health-care-irc-section-501r), a tax-exempt hospital organization must establish a written **Financial Assistance Policy (FAP)** for each hospital facility it operates.

The IRS says the policy must apply to emergency and other medically necessary care and must explain eligibility criteria, available assistance, how to apply, and other required information. Hospitals must also make the policy widely available.

Ask:

> "Please send me your Financial Assistance Policy, the plain-language summary, and the application."

Don't decide you're ineligible based on income alone. The hospital's own policy is the document that tells you its eligibility criteria and what assistance it offers.

The [IRS also requires the plain-language summary](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/financial-assistance-policy-and-emergency-medical-care-policy-section-501r4) to explain the eligibility requirements and assistance available, how to apply, and where to obtain the policy and application.

### What happens if you qualify?

For a person who is eligible for assistance under a tax-exempt hospital's FAP, Section 501(r) limits what the hospital may charge for emergency or other medically necessary care. The IRS describes this as a limit tied to the amounts generally billed (AGB) to individuals who have insurance covering that care.

The details matter, including the hospital's method for calculating AGB, so read the hospital's FAP rather than relying on a generic percentage.

Also remember that not every discount a hospital offers is necessarily part of its FAP. The IRS distinguishes financial assistance under the FAP from other discounts, such as self-pay or prompt-pay discounts.

## Step 4: Compare the charge with the hospital's published prices

Hospital price transparency can give you a useful benchmark.

CMS requires hospitals to make standard charge information publicly available. This is why Unblind Health exists. [Check each CPT code and NPI](https://unblindhealth.com/procedures) to find out information about negotiated charges and discounted cash prices. If NPI code is missing on your bill, you can find it at [NPI registry](https://npiregistry.cms.hhs.gov/search).

The federal price-transparency requirements were updated for 2026. [CMS's 2026 guidance](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/mln7215754-hospital-price-transparency.pdf) includes additional requirements concerning actual-dollar allowed amounts in hospital machine-readable files in certain circumstances.

That means there can be more useful information to compare than simply the amount printed on your bill.

Instead of saying:

> "This seems expensive."

you can ask:

> "I reviewed the hospital's published pricing information. Can you explain how the amount on my bill was calculated and how it compares with the applicable published rate?"

The comparison is not always straightforward. Pricing files can be technical, and the same service can have different negotiated amounts depending on the insurer, plan, service setting, or billing circumstances.

But a published benchmark can give you a more concrete starting point for a conversation with billing.

## Step 5: Ask about the discounted cash price

If you're uninsured, self-paying, or considering paying outside your insurance, ask whether the hospital offers a discounted cash price.

Try:

> "What is the discounted cash price for this service?"

You can also ask whether there is an additional discount for paying the balance in full.

Don't offer to pay in full unless you can comfortably do so.

If you have insurance, ask your insurer how a cash payment would affect your deductible or out-of-pocket costs before agreeing to a cash arrangement. Your plan's terms matter.

## Step 6: Ask about a payment plan

If you can't afford the balance, ask what payment arrangements the hospital offers.

Before agreeing, ask:

- Is there interest?
- Are there fees?
- What happens if a payment is late?
- Can I still apply for financial assistance?
- What happens to the account while I'm making payments?
- Is a third-party financing company involved?

A hospital payment plan and a medical financing product are not necessarily the same thing. Read the agreement before signing it.

## Step 7: Put important requests in writing

Phone calls can help you get answers quickly. Written records make it easier to keep track of what was requested and what was agreed.

If you are disputing a charge, requesting financial assistance, asking for a discount, or proposing a payment arrangement, send a short written request.

Include:

1. Your account number
2. The date of service
3. The charge or balance you're questioning
4. What you are asking the hospital to do
5. Supporting documents, if relevant
6. A request for a written response

Keep copies of your correspondence.

It can also help to keep a simple call log:

- Date
- Time
- Department
- Representative's name
- What you discussed
- What happens next

If the hospital's patient portal provides a dated message history, that can also be useful for keeping a record.



## What should you actually say?

You don't need to sound like a lawyer. You need to be clear about what you're asking.

### When requesting an itemized bill

> "I'd like a fully itemized statement showing the individual charges and billing codes."

### When questioning a charge

> "Can you explain this charge? I don't believe I received this service."

### When requesting financial assistance

> "Please send me your Financial Assistance Policy, plain-language summary, and application."

### When asking about the price

> "Can you explain how this amount was calculated, and what published pricing information applies to this service?"

### When asking about a discount

> "Are there any financial-assistance, self-pay, or other discounts that apply to this balance?"

### When you can't afford the balance

> "I want to resolve the balance, but I can't afford the current amount. What financial-assistance or payment-plan options are available?"

The goal isn't to win an argument with the person answering the phone. It's to get the bill reviewed through the right process.

## What not to do

### Don't assume the first amount is the only amount that matters

First understand what the charge represents, how it was calculated, and whether insurance or financial assistance changes the amount you are responsible for.

### Don't assume you earn too much for financial assistance

Check the hospital's Financial Assistance Policy.

### Don't assume a payment plan is the same as financing

Read the terms, including interest and fees.

### Don't rely on verbal promises for important changes

If someone agrees to reduce, waive, or restructure a balance, ask for the agreement in writing.

### Don't ignore the bill

Reviewing a bill is different from refusing to deal with it. Ask about deadlines and collection policies while you work through the review.

## How much can a hospital bill be reduced?

There isn't a single reduction percentage that applies to hospital bills.

A balance may change because of:

- A billing correction
- An insurance claim correction
- Financial assistance
- A self-pay or other hospital discount
- A surprise-billing protection
- A payment arrangement
- Another adjustment available under the hospital's policies

The amount depends on the circumstances. Be skeptical of anyone promising that every hospital bill can be reduced by a particular percentage.

## Does medical debt affect your credit?

Be careful with older articles on this topic.

The [CFPB issued a federal medical-debt credit-reporting rule](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-finalizes-rule-to-remove-medical-bills-from-credit-reports/) in January 2025. On July 11, 2025, a federal court vacated that rule. The CFPB's current materials identify the rule as vacated and archived.

So the 2025 federal rule should not be described as a current federal ban on medical debt appearing in credit reports.

Credit reporting can also involve state law and credit-bureau policies. If a medical account has gone to collections or you are concerned about a credit report, check the rules that apply to your situation.

## The bottom line

If you're searching for how to negotiate a hospital bill, don't start with:

**"How do I get them to give me a discount?"**

Start with:

**"Is this bill correct, and what options apply to my situation?"**

Then work through the possibilities:

1. Get the itemized bill.
2. Review the individual charges.
3. Check whether a surprise-billing protection applies.
4. Read the hospital's Financial Assistance Policy.
5. Compare the charge with available hospital pricing information.
6. Ask about the discounted cash price if relevant.
7. Ask about payment options if you can't afford the balance.
8. Keep important requests and agreements in writing.

You don't need to approach the hospital billing department as an adversary. You do need enough information to understand what you're being asked to pay and which options are available.

If the bill is unaffordable, ask about financial assistance and payment options before taking on another form of debt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you negotiate a hospital bill?

Sometimes. Depending on the situation, you may be able to correct a billing error, qualify for financial assistance, use a billing dispute process, ask about a discount, or arrange a payment plan.

### How do I ask a hospital to lower my bill?

Start by requesting an itemized bill. Then check for errors, review the hospital's Financial Assistance Policy, and ask about applicable discounts or payment arrangements. If you are questioning a particular charge, explain the charge and provide supporting information when available.

### What is hospital financial assistance?

Hospital financial assistance is free or discounted care provided to people who meet a hospital's eligibility criteria and cannot pay all or part of the cost. Tax-exempt hospital organizations are required under Section 501(r) to maintain a written Financial Assistance Policy.

### How do I find a hospital's financial assistance policy?

Search the hospital's website for "Financial Assistance Policy," "Financial Assistance," or "Charity Care." You can also ask the billing department, financial counselor, or patient financial services department for the policy, its plain-language summary, and the application.

### What if I don't have insurance?

Ask about the hospital's self-pay or uninsured pricing and request a Good Faith Estimate for scheduled services when applicable. Under the No Surprises Act, an uninsured or self-pay patient may have a federal dispute option if a provider's final bill is at least $400 more than the provider's Good Faith Estimate.

### Should I pay a hospital bill before reviewing it?

It is reasonable to review the bill and check for financial assistance or applicable protections before paying. At the same time, don't ignore the account. Ask the hospital about deadlines, payment options, and collection policies while you review it.

### How much can a hospital bill be reduced?

There is no standard percentage. The amount, if any, depends on the reason for the adjustment, the hospital's policies, insurance, financial-assistance eligibility, and other circumstances.

### What is the best way to negotiate a medical bill?

Bring specific information to the conversation: the itemized bill, relevant insurance documents, the hospital's financial-assistance policy, published pricing information when available, and a written record of your communications.
