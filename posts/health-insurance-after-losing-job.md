If you lose job-based health insurance, **do not automatically choose COBRA**. Depending on your income, family situation, age, and health needs, Medicaid, a spouse's plan, or an ACA Marketplace plan may cost less.

There is no universal cheapest plan. The useful question is: **which options should you price first?**

## What should you check first after losing job-based coverage?

**Start with the options that may be subsidized, then compare COBRA against the real price of those alternatives.**

| Check first | Coverage option | Why it belongs on the list | Where to start |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Medicaid / CHIP | Can be free or low-cost if your income falls enough | [Find your state program](https://www.medicaid.gov/about-us/where-can-people-get-help-medicaid-chip) |
| **2** | Spouse's or another employer plan | The employer may pay part of the premium | Ask HR or the benefits team |
| **3** | ACA Marketplace plan | Premium tax credits may reduce what you pay | [Find your Marketplace](https://www.healthcare.gov/marketplace-in-your-state/) |
| **4** | Bronze plan + HSA | Lower-premium option worth comparing in 2026 | [Compare Marketplace plans](https://www.healthcare.gov/see-plans/) |
| **5** | Catastrophic plan, if eligible | Lower-premium protection against large bills | [Check Catastrophic eligibility](https://www.healthcare.gov/choose-a-plan/catastrophic-health-plans/) |
| **6** | COBRA with employer subsidy | Severance can change the math dramatically | Ask what your former employer will pay |
| **7** | Full-price ACA plan | Comprehensive coverage even without a tax credit | [Shop Marketplace plans](https://www.healthcare.gov/see-plans/) |
| **8** | COBRA without subsidy | You may have to pay nearly the full plan cost | Review your COBRA election notice |
| **Separate** | Short-term coverage | Temporary coverage with different protections and exclusions | Check the policy and your state rules |

Medicare is separate from this ranking because it applies only if you are already eligible.

## What should you do first?

**Find out the exact date your current coverage ends.** Your last day at work and your last day of health insurance are not always the same.

Check your benefits or termination paperwork, or ask HR: **“What is the last day my health insurance is active?”**

That date drives several enrollment deadlines. The Marketplace generally gives you a window before and after loss of qualifying coverage, while another employer plan such as a spouse's plan can have a shorter deadline.

See [Special Enrollment After Losing Health Insurance](https://unblindhealth.com/blog/special-enrollment-after-losing-health-insurance) for the timing details.

## Can you join a spouse's employer plan?

**Often, yes — and it is one of the first prices worth checking.** If you are eligible for another employer plan, the employer contribution can make it much cheaper than individual coverage.

Ask for four numbers or answers:

- What will the payroll deduction be after adding me or the family?
- When would coverage start?
- What are the deductible and out-of-pocket maximum?
- Are our doctors and prescriptions covered?

You do not need to decide whether the plan is “good” before getting those answers. First get the actual cost.

## Is a Marketplace plan cheaper than COBRA?

**It can be, especially if your household qualifies for a premium tax credit.** Marketplace savings are based on your expected household income for the year, so a job loss can materially change the price you see.

Do not use your old salary as a shortcut. And do not enter only what you expect to earn after the layoff. Estimate the household income the Marketplace asks for for the full coverage year.

If you are deciding specifically between these two routes, use [COBRA vs Marketplace](https://unblindhealth.com/blog/cobra-vs-marketplace) rather than re-comparing them here.

## When does COBRA deserve a serious look?

**COBRA can be worth the higher premium when keeping the same plan protects money you have already spent or avoids disruption in care.**

It deserves a closer look if:

- your former employer is subsidizing it as part of severance;
- you have already met much of your deductible or out-of-pocket maximum;
- you are in ongoing treatment;
- keeping the same doctors, hospital system, or prescription coverage matters.

COBRA generally lets you stay on the same group plan, but you may have to pay the employee share, the former employer's share, and up to a 2% administrative charge.

For the price mechanics, see [How Much Does COBRA Cost?](https://unblindhealth.com/blog/how-much-does-cobra-cost).

## Should you compare Bronze or Catastrophic coverage?

**Yes, particularly if your priority is a lower monthly premium — but compare the net premium and financial risk, not the metal label alone.**

For 2026, Bronze and Catastrophic plans can work with HSAs under the new federal rules. Catastrophic plans, however, have additional eligibility restrictions and cannot use Marketplace premium tax credits or cost-sharing reductions.

That means a Bronze or Silver plan can sometimes beat a Catastrophic plan once subsidies are included.

See [Catastrophic Health Insurance: Who Qualifies and Is It Worth It?](https://unblindhealth.com/blog/catastrophic-health-insurance).

## What if you become self-employed?

**You can generally use the individual Marketplace if you become a freelancer, consultant, or solo business owner without employees.** Marketplace savings are based on your expected household income, including your estimate of net self-employment income.

That is a separate decision from whether you may qualify for a self-employed health insurance tax deduction.

See [Health Insurance for Self-Employed People](https://unblindhealth.com/blog/health-insurance-for-self-employed).

## Where does short-term insurance fit?

**Treat short-term insurance as temporary gap coverage, not as a cheaper version of an ACA Marketplace plan.** It can have different exclusions, underwriting rules, benefits, and consumer protections.

If you have a known short gap before new employer coverage starts, it may be one option to compare. But first understand exactly what the policy covers and excludes.

See [Short-Term Health Insurance: What to Check Before You Buy](https://unblindhealth.com/blog/short-term-health-insurance).

## How should you make the final decision?

**Compare the cost of a normal year and the cost of a bad year.** For each realistic option, write down:

1. annual premium after any employer contribution or tax credit;
2. deductible;
3. out-of-pocket maximum for covered in-network care;
4. deductible or out-of-pocket progress you would lose by switching;
5. expected prescription and recurring-care costs;
6. whether your doctors, hospitals, and medications are covered.

That one-page comparison is usually more useful than ranking plans by premium alone.

## Bottom line

After losing job-based insurance, start with **Medicaid/CHIP, another employer plan, and the ACA Marketplace before defaulting to COBRA**.

COBRA may still be the better financial choice if it is subsidized, you have already spent heavily under the plan, or continuity of care matters enough to change the math.

The goal is not to find the plan that sounds cheapest. It is to **see the options, price them, and choose the tradeoff that fits your household** — the same idea behind [Unblind Health](https://unblindhealth.com/about-unblind-health).
